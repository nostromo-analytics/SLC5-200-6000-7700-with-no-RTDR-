A 6,000 ton-hr system on a differential discharge saves more per year than a 7,700 on a fixed setpoint — and costs $1.77M less to build.

$407,000 against $399,500, for $6.23M instead of $8.00M. NPV roughly doubles, from $776,000 to $1.54M; SIR goes from 1.25 to 1.65.

Both effects are real but they are different in kind. Dispatch is free — the same ice, spent to hold a flat ceiling instead of subtracting a constant, is worth +$52,000 a year at 6,000 ton-hr and +$43,600 at 7,700. Size is not free: every extra ton-hr costs $1,039 and buys progressively less, because the demand-response income that carries most of the case is set by the 2,000-ton discharge capacity, not by how much ice sits behind it.

Run the sizing sweep at the bottom and the return keeps climbing all the way down. That is a finding about the DR programme, not about ice — and it is the number to interrogate before committing to a size.

Four scenarios

Same tariff, same load, same simulator

The fixed-setpoint row is the workbook's own control strategy: discharge storage ÷ 7 tons every on-peak hour. At 7,700 ton-hr that is exactly the 1,100 tons the workbook models, and it reproduces the workbook's demand and DR figures. The differential row spends the same daily ice budget to hold a flat kW ceiling.

Dispatch beats size. Going from 6,000 to 7,700 ton-hr costs $1.77M and adds $36,000 a year on the differential dispatch. Going from a fixed setpoint to a differential one costs nothing and adds $52,000 a year at 6,000 ton-hr. If only one change gets made, it is not the tonnage.

How the return moves with size

1,000 – 12,000 ton-hr, 250 ton-hr steps

Every size run through the same simulator, priced on the workbook's own linear $1,039 / ton-hr and its own incentive stack. Annual savings rise with size; returns fall with size, and they fall monotonically.

Annual savings by storage size

Table view
Read this curve with the DR programme in mind. $157,000 of the annual saving is a full-participation demand-response payment that depends only on the 2,000-ton discharge capacity — it is identical at 1,000 ton-hr and at 12,000. That fixed block divided by a capex that scales linearly is what makes small systems look unbeatable. The green line adds the one physical constraint the workbook omits: a system cannot answer a 45-minute DR event at 2,000 tons unless it holds 1,500 ton-hr in reserve. Even with that floor the return still peaks at 2,500 ton-hr.

What this says

My read
Do this first

Fix the dispatch before touching the size

A differential discharge is worth +$52,000/yr at 6,000 ton-hr and +$43,600/yr at 7,700, for no capital and no equipment change. It peaks at 1,417 tons at 6,000 ton-hr and 1,592 at 7,700 — both inside the 2,000-ton discharge capacity already installed — on the same daily ice budget and the same 8-hour charge window.

It also cuts waste: at 6,000 ton-hr a differential dispatch adds 195 MWh/yr of consumption against the fixed setpoint's 625 MWh, because it stops spending ice on hours that never set the bill.

Every sizing conclusion below assumes it. Comparing sizes on a fixed setpoint compares two systems that are both being run badly.

Sizing

6,000 dominates 7,700 on every return metric

On the differential dispatch, 6,000 ton-hr returns 5.81-year payback, SIR 1.65, NPV $1.54M against the 7,700's 6.86 years, 1.40 and $1.21M. The 7,700 buys $36,000 more per year for $1.77M more capital — a marginal return of about 2% a year before incentives.

If the goal is absolute kW removed from the bill, the 7,700 wins. If it is return on capital, it does not.

Structural

Duration binds the optimised dispatch, not peak height

With a fixed setpoint the hottest day is always the binding day. Once you level-shave, the binding day becomes the one whose on-peak block is broadest — a wide block exhausts the daily ice budget before it reaches a low ceiling.

At both sizes it moves the binding day in the same three months — January, May and June. Those are the days a real design study should size against, and they are not the ones a peak-load table points to.

The question behind the curve

The sizing answer is really a demand-response answer

Across the whole sweep, DR income moves only from $219,000 down to $160,000 while capex goes from $1.0M to $12.5M — and it moves down, because deeper shaving eats the partial-participation term. Everything the extra tonnage buys is tariff demand charge, and that is the expensive half.

So the sizing decision reduces to a judgement the model cannot make: how much of the case do you want resting on an RMP programme whose terms can change? A 7,700 is the hedge; a 2,500 is the return-maximiser. The 6,000 sits between them.

Price caveat

The sweep inherits a linear price that probably isn't

Every point uses $1,039 / ton-hr, the workbook's own basis, scaled from the full SLC5 design. Chillers, heat exchangers and piping do not scale linearly, so the small end of the curve is flattered and the large end is penalised.

A bottom-up quote at 6,000 ton-hr would move the whole answer. It is the single input most worth replacing with a real number.

Caveats

Three things the dispatch model does not capture

Perfect foresight. The ceiling is set from a known TMY3 year. A real controller sees a forecast and must hold margin — expect to give back some of the gain.

Constant pump power. A flat 78.33 kW is charged whenever discharging, regardless of rate. At 1,592 tons rather than 1,100 the real parasitic load is higher; on affinity-law scaling roughly 20–30 kW in the peak hours.

Charge-before-use. The workbook charges the day's ice after it is discharged. I kept that convention so every case is comparable.

Method. The 8,760-hour model was extracted from the workbook and re-implemented. At 7,700 ton-hr on a fixed 1,100-ton setpoint it reproduces the workbook exactly on monthly billed demand, demand-response savings and net cost after incentives; total annual savings come to $399,473 against the workbook's $398,704. The $769 difference is a workbook artefact — the charge pump draws 27.975 kW on weekend nights when no ice was used that day. Every scenario here runs through the same simulator, so all comparisons are internally consistent.

Optimisation. For each weekday, discharge is solved by bisection for the lowest flat kW ceiling reachable subject to the daily ton-hr budget, 2,000 tons per hour, and never discharging more than the hour's load. The month's billed demand is then the highest of its weekdays' ceilings, and every day is shaved to that ceiling and no further.

Economics. Cost is $1,038.99 / ton-hr; net cost is gross less the 40% federal credit, 21% depreciation and an RMP incentive of $50 per kW of peak demand removed. NPV and SIR use the 15-year present-value factor implied by the workbook's own 8% discount rate and 2% savings escalation. Demand-response income follows the workbook's formula: full participation on 68.75% of events at the site's 2,286 kW response, partial participation on the rest, net of what peak shaving has already taken.
